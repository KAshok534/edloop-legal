## Most-asked topics

The chapter divides across four primary hydrocarbon classes:

- Alkenes: The highest-volume topic with 120 total questions (93 in 2019 to 2026), examined continuously through 2026. Key areas test Markovnikov addition of hydrogen halides, peroxide-induced free radical additions (Kharasch effect), anti-addition of halogens via cyclic halonium ions, reductive and oxidative ozonolysis to identify double bond positions, and hydroboration-oxidation versus oxymercuration-demercuration.
- Aromatic Hydrocarbons: Generated 87 total questions (74 in 2019 to 2026), tested through 2026. Focuses on electrophilic aromatic substitution mechanisms on benzene and substituted benzenes, ortho/para versus meta directing effects, activating versus deactivating substituent influences on nitration and halogenation rates, and limitations of Friedel-Crafts reactions on strongly deactivated rings.
- Alkynes: Produced 40 total questions (29 in 2019 to 2026), active through 2026. Core concepts evaluate terminal alkyne acetylide formation with sodamide, stereoselective hydrogenations (cis-alkenes via Lindlar palladium catalyst versus trans-alkenes via sodium in liquid ammonia), and mercuric ion catalyzed hydration producing ketones.
- Alkanes: Yielded 40 total questions (28 in 2019 to 2026), evaluated through 2026. Coverage includes free radical chlorination and bromination selectivities, Wurtz reaction coupling limitations, decarboxylation of carboxylic acids, and controlled catalytic oxidations.

## What the questions look like

Examiners regularly present multi-step synthesis roadmaps. A typical question starts with an alkyne, treats it with sodium amide and an alkyl halide to extend the chain, reduces it stereoselectively using Lindlar catalyst or Birch reduction, and follows with halogen addition or ozonolysis to identify the final product.

In aromatic chemistry, questions frequently ask students to compare relative rates of electrophilic aromatic substitution across anisole, toluene, chlorobenzene, and nitrobenzene. Ozonolysis problems require working backward from fragments (such as glyoxal, acetone, or formaldehyde) to reconstruct the starting symmetrical or unsymmetrical diene.

## How to practise it

Because Hydrocarbons ranks third in overall chemistry weight, focus on mechanism distinctions and reagent catalogs:

1. Master alkene stereospecific additions: Know that addition of bromine to trans-alkenes yields meso products, while addition to cis-alkenes produces racemic enantiomeric pairs.
2. Differentiate alkyne reduction reagents: Remember that H₂ with Lindlar catalyst (Pd/CaCO₃ poisoned with quinoline) delivers syn addition yielding cis-alkenes, while sodium in liquid ammonia yields trans-alkenes via radical anions.
3. Master directing effects on benzene: Activating groups and halogens are ortho/para directing; deactivating groups (except halogens) are meta directing. Halogens are unique because they are deactivating yet ortho/para directing due to competing resonance and induction.
4. Practise reverse ozonolysis: Cleave C=O double bonds on paper and fuse the carbons back together with a C=C double bond to determine original alkene structures.
5. Review terminal alkyne acidity: Terminal alkynes react with sodamide or ammoniacal cuprous chloride, whereas internal alkynes do not react.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which alkene hydration method gives anti-Markovnikov alcohol?
Hydroboration-oxidation adds water in an overall anti-Markovnikov sense without carbocation rearrangement, producing primary alcohols from terminal alkenes.

### Why do halobenzenes undergo electrophilic substitution at ortho and para positions despite being deactivated?
Halogens possess strong negative inductive effects that withdraw overall electron density from the ring, making them deactivating. However, unshared lone pairs donate electron density into ortho and para positions through resonance, directing incoming electrophiles to those sites.

### Can alkanes with odd numbers of carbons be prepared efficiently by the Wurtz reaction?
No, using two different alkyl halides in a Wurtz reaction produces a statistical mixture of three different alkanes that have very close boiling points and are exceedingly difficult to separate. Step-by-step solutions for each one are in the EdLoop app.
