The syllabus organizes across three fundamental topics:

- Amines: The largest topic with 134 total questions (111 in 2019 to 2026), evaluated continuously through 2026. Major areas evaluate aqueous basicity sequences (interplay of inductive, hydration, and steric effects), Hinsberg reagent (benzenesulfonyl chloride) distinguishing primary, secondary, and tertiary amines, carbylamine test for primary amines, acylation (protecting amino groups during bromination or nitration of aniline), and Gabriel phthalimide synthesis.

Centers on selective reduction of nitro groups (ammonium sulfide Zinin reduction), catalytic hydrogenation of nitriles to primary amines, and acidic versus alkaline hydrolysis of alkyl cyanides and isocyanides.

## What the questions look like

Examiners regularly evaluate reaction sequences revolving around aniline. A typical problem begins with nitrobenzene, reduces it with tin or iron in hydrochloric acid to aniline, protects the amino group with acetic anhydride to prevent polybromination, carries out monobromination at the para position, and hydrolyzes the anilide back to para-bromoaniline.

Diazonium problems frequently test synthetic routes to meta-substituted aromatics that cannot be prepared by direct electrophilic aromatic substitution, such as converting toluene via nitration, reduction, diazotization, and Sandmeyer reaction into 3,5-dibromotoluene.

## How to practise it

Mastering nitrogen chemistry requires memorizing diagnostic tests and protecting strategies:

1. Memorize aqueous basicity orders: In aqueous solution, ethyl-substituted amines follow secondary > tertiary > primary > ammonia; methyl-substituted amines follow secondary > primary > tertiary > ammonia due to steric hindrance and hydration energies.
2. Master the Hinsberg test: Primary amines react with benzenesulfonyl chloride to give sulfonamides with an acidic N–H hydrogen, which dissolve in aqueous potassium hydroxide; secondary amines give sulfonamides without an acidic hydrogen, remaining insoluble in alkali; tertiary amines do not react.
3. Understand Gabriel phthalimide constraints: It prepares pure primary aliphatic amines exclusively; aryl halides cannot undergo the necessary SN2 displacement on the phthalimide anion, so aniline cannot be prepared this way.
4. Note Hofmann bromamide degradation: Treats an amide with bromine and alkali, releasing a primary amine containing exactly one fewer carbon atom via an isocyanate intermediate.
5. Review diazonium replacements: H₃PO₂ or CH₃CH₂OH reduces the diazonium group to a hydrogen atom, providing a classic method for removing an amino directing group from an aromatic ring.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is direct nitration of aniline problematic?
Aniline is readily oxidized by strong nitric acid into complex tarry mixtures. Furthermore, in strong acid, aniline gets protonated into the meta-directing anilinium ion, giving an unusually large amount of meta-nitroaniline alongside ortho and para isomers unless protected by acetylation.

### Why does Gabriel phthalimide synthesis fail for aromatic amines?
The reaction requires nucleophilic displacement (SN2) of a halide by the potassium phthalimide anion. Aryl halides cannot undergo SN2 displacement due to resonance partial double-bond character and steric shielding of the aromatic ring.

### How does the carbylamine test work?
Heating any aliphatic or aromatic primary amine with chloroform and alcoholic potassium hydroxide produces an extremely foul-smelling carbylamine (isocyanide), providing a conclusive test for primary amines. Step-by-step solutions for each one are in the EdLoop app.
