## How often it comes up

A dedicated straight line question appears in approximately three out of every four examination shifts. Between 2019 and 2026, 125 shifts included problems from this unit. In the 2024, 2025, and 2026 cycles, the question counts were 30, 22, and 16 questions respectively.

Because lines define the sides of triangles, chords of circles, and axes of symmetry, straight line mechanics permeate all of coordinate geometry. Even when an exam problem is categorized under circles or parabolas, the core algebraic workload frequently reduces to line perpendicularity, distance formulas, or angle bisectors.

## Most-asked topics

Questions in this chapter divide between two large primary topics and one historical subtopic:

1. **Equations of Lines (83 questions, 2019-2026; 119 total, last asked 2026)**
The largest concentration of questions involves slope forms, intercept forms, parametric or symmetric line equations, distance between parallel lines, perpendicular distance from a point to a line, and equations of angle bisectors.

2. **Points & Basics (73 questions, 2019-2026; 101 total, last asked 2026)**
Deals with triangle geometry in coordinates, including the centroid, incentre, circumcentre, and orthocentre. Many questions ask students to find the image of a point or the foot of a perpendicular across a given line, or determine collinearity and concurrency conditions.

3. **Pair of Straight Lines (3 questions, 2019-2026; 10 total, last asked 2023)**
Covers homogeneous and general second-degree equations representing pairs of intersecting or parallel lines, angle bisectors of line pairs, and lines joining the origin to intersection points. This topic was officially removed in recent syllabus revisions.

## What the questions look like

Examiners repeatedly deploy several classical coordinate configurations:

- **Triangle Centres and Orthocentre Relations**: Given two vertices of a triangle and its orthocentre or circumcentre, compute the coordinates of the third vertex using perpendicular slope relations and section formulas.
- **Parametric Distance Along a Line**: A line drawn in a specific angular direction from a known point intersects another line or circle. Using the parametric form x equals x1 plus r cos theta allows distances along the ray to be found cleanly.
- **Angle Bisectors and Reflections**: Determining the bisector of the acute or obtuse angle between two intersecting lines, or identifying the image of a given point in a reflecting line mirror.
- **Perpendicular Distance and Parallel Strips**: Finding locus points or triangle areas bounded between parallel lines separated by specified perpendicular offsets.

## How to practise it

Fluency in straight lines depends on using coordinate shortcuts rather than raw algebra:

- **Use the parametric symmetric form**: Whenever a problem mentions distance measured along a line inclined at an angle, express coordinates as x1 plus r cos theta and y1 plus r sin theta. Substituting these directly into the target curve or line yields a simple quadratic or linear equation in r.
- **Master the foot of the perpendicular formula**: Memorise the standard coordinate formulas for the foot of the perpendicular and the reflection image of a point across the line ax plus by plus c equals zero. Deriving them from scratch during the exam wastes critical minutes.
- **Exploit Euler's line relationship**: In any non-equilateral triangle, remember that the orthocentre, centroid, and circumcentre are collinear, with the centroid dividing the distance between orthocentre and circumcentre in the ratio two to one. This property frequently shortcuts vertex computations.
- **Differentiate acute and obtuse bisectors**: Write both line equations with positive constant terms. Evaluate the sign of a1 a2 plus b1 b2. If it is positive, the positive sign yields the obtuse angle bisector; if negative, the positive sign corresponds to the acute angle bisector.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the pair of straight lines topic still part of the syllabus?
General and homogeneous pairs of straight lines passing through the origin were dropped from the official JEE Main syllabus starting in 2024. Questions specifically asking for angles between pairs of lines or joint equations have stopped appearing, though understanding individual straight lines remains paramount.

### How do I identify whether an angle bisector contains the origin?
Write the equations of both intersecting lines such that their constant terms are both strictly positive. The bisector formed with the positive sign between the normalized equations is the bisector of the angle containing the origin.

### Why is the parametric form of a line so widely tested?
The parametric line formulation converts geometric questions involving lengths and distances into purely algebraic roots of one scalar variable r. This avoids cumbersome square roots from distance formulas and connects smoothly with polynomial equations. Step-by-step solutions for each one are in the EdLoop app.
