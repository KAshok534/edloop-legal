## What the questions look like

Examiners test bounded area using recurring geometric formats:

- **Set-Theoretic Inequality Specifications**: The target region is presented as a set of coordinate pairs satisfying simultaneous algebraic inequalities, such as a quadratic boundary and a linear boundary, requiring students to shade and isolate the valid intersection.
- **Conic and Modulus Intersections**: Regions lying inside an ellipse or circle while simultaneously remaining outside a square oriented with vertices along the coordinate axes.
- **Parabola and Line Partitioning**: A parabola is sliced by a line passing through given intercepts, and the problem asks for the area enclosed between them or the ratio into which a vertical line divides the region.
- **Symmetric Two-Curve Enclosures**: Finding the common area enclosed between two orthogonal parabolas or between an inverted parabola and a modulus V-shape.

## How to practise it

Accurate area calculation requires visualising the geometry before integrating:

- **Sketch the curves immediately**: Always draw a quick rough sketch showing the orientations, axes of symmetry, and vertices of the intersecting curves. Visually identify the enclosed area to avoid integrating over the wrong branch or missing disconnected lobes.
- **Compute intersection points precisely**: Equate the equations of the bounding curves and solve for their intersection points. Double-check extraneous roots, particularly when squaring equations or handling piecewise absolute value functions.
- **Choose the integration axis wisely**: Decide whether integrating with respect to x or y yields a simpler calculation. If a horizontal strip connects a single left curve to a single right curve throughout the region, integrating with respect to y eliminates the need to split the integral into multiple piecewise intervals.
- **Exploit geometric symmetry**: If a region possesses symmetry about the coordinate axes or the origin, compute the area of one symmetric sector and scale it appropriately. This reduces computational workload and lowers the chance of sign mistakes during limit substitution.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I integrate along the x-axis or the y-axis?
Inspect the boundaries of the shaded region. If the top boundary or bottom boundary changes functional form midway across the interval, integrating along x requires splitting the region into two or more separate integrals. In such situations, expressing x as a function of y and integrating with respect to y often allows the entire area to be evaluated as a single integral from the lower y-limit to the upper y-limit.

### What is Archimedes' formula for a parabola and a chord?
For the area enclosed between a parabola and a secant line connecting two points on it, Archimedes proved that the enclosed area equals two-thirds of the area of the circumscribed parallelogram, or two-thirds of the base chord multiplied by the perpendicular height. While useful for rapid sanity checks, setting up the exact definite integral remains the safest approach for JEE Main.

### How do I handle regions defined by inequalities?
Treat each inequality initially as an equation to draw the boundary curve. Then test a convenient test point not lying on the curve, such as the origin, to determine which side of the boundary curve satisfies the inequality.
