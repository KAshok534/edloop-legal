Sets, Relations and Functions is one of the most frequently tested chapters in JEE Main Mathematics. In the papers we hold from 2019 to 2026, it gave **266 questions in 162 papers**, about **1.6 questions per paper**, and it appeared in 153 of those papers. By share of Mathematics questions (5.8%) it ranks **2nd of 29 Mathematics chapters** in that period.

The chapter has grown significantly since the AIEEE years. From 2002 to 2018 it was 4.2% of Mathematics, with 1.4 questions per paper; in the last three exam years (2024 to 2026) it rose to 7.6%, with 118 questions across the 58 papers we hold. The busiest year in our set was 2023, with 50 questions across 24 papers. Since 2019 there has been no year without it, appearing in 153 of the 162 papers we hold.

It is a Class 11 chapter, part of NTA's Unit 1: Sets, Relations and Functions.

## Most-asked topics

Three topics cover all the questions. From 2019 to 2026:

- **Functions**: 170 questions. The dominant topic by a large margin.
- **Relations**: 70 questions. Equivalence, reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity come up repeatedly.
- **Sets**: 26 questions. Union, intersection and complement through inequalities.

All three topics appeared in 2026.

## What the questions look like

Functions questions fall into two main types. The first asks for the domain of a composite or nested expression involving logarithms, square roots and inverse trigonometric functions together; you have to satisfy several constraints at once. The second asks you to count functions with a given property, such as strictly increasing maps from one finite set to another where no value maps to itself.

Relations questions nearly always give a rule like "2x + 3y is an integer" or "the difference of x and y is a multiple of 3" on a small finite set and ask how many ordered pairs the relation contains, or how many pairs must be added to make it symmetric, or whether it is an equivalence relation.

Sets questions typically define two or three sets through polynomial or absolute-value inequalities and ask which set operation on them equals a given description.

Of the 266 questions since 2019, 62 were numerical-answer questions with no options, so exact counting and careful logic matter as much as multiple-choice elimination.

## How to practise it

Start with functions, since it carries most of the marks. Practise finding domains systematically: list every constraint, solve each one, and take the intersection. Pay special attention to composite expressions where the output of one function must lie in the domain of the next.

Then move to relations. Work through the conditions for reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity on small sets until you can verify or disprove each one quickly. Counting questions about relations benefit from a careful enumeration rather than a formula approach.

Leave Sets for last; there are fewer questions and they respond well to number-line sketches that show the intersection and union of solution sets.
