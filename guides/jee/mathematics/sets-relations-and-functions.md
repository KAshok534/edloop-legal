## What the questions look like

Examiners test both discrete relation counting and continuous algebraic analysis. A classic relation question defines an algebraic rule on a finite set of integers and asks how many ordered pairs must be added or removed to achieve symmetry, reflexivity, or transitivity.

In functions, problems regularly require finding the intersection of validity domains for square roots, fractional denominators, and logarithmic arguments. Other common problems present functional equations (such as f(x+y) = f(x)f(y) yielding an exponential function) and ask for the sum of a finite series over the integers.

## How to practise it

Because this unit ranks second in mathematics weight, master discrete logic alongside continuous function manipulation:

1. Master relation properties: Check reflexivity (does (a,a) belong for all elements?), symmetry (does (a,b) imply (b,a)?), and transitivity (do (a,b) and (b,c) imply (a,c)?). Pay extra attention to vacuous transitivity when no chaining pair exists.
2. Calculate domains methodically: Break composite expressions into simultaneous inequalities: radicands of even roots must be non-negative; denominators cannot equal zero; logarithmic arguments must be strictly positive with positive bases not equal to one.
3. Test injectivity and surjectivity: For injectivity, verify whether f'(x) maintains a single sign on the domain; for surjectivity, verify whether the calculated range equals the stated codomain.
4. Recognize standard functional equations: f(x+y) = f(x)f(y) implies f(x) = a^x; f(xy) = f(x) + f(y) implies f(x) = k ln(x); f(x+y) = f(x) + f(y) implies f(x) = kx.
5. Practise counting mappings: Review combinations and binomial coefficients used to count total functions, one-one functions, and onto functions between finite sets.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why has Sets, Relations & Functions grown to over seven percent of recent mathematics papers?
The unit serves as the essential gateway to both calculus and abstract algebra, allowing examiners to test logical rigor, inequality solving, and combinatorics within a single question.

### When is a relation transitive if an element has no chain partner?
A relation is transitive unless there exists a specific counterexample where (a,b) and (b,c) belong to the relation but (a,c) is absent. If no such pair (a,b) and (b,c) exists to begin with, the condition holds vacuously.
