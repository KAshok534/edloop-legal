## Most-asked topics

Questions in this chapter group into four distinct topics:

1. **Maxima & Minima (95 questions, 2019-2026; 125 total, last asked 2026)**
The dominant topic in the chapter. Problems require determining local extrema, global extrema on closed intervals, critical points where derivatives vanish or fail to exist, and optimization of geometric structures such as cones, cylinders, and rectangles.

2. **Monotonicity (40 questions, 2019-2026; 48 total, last asked 2026)**
Focuses on intervals where functions strictly increase or decrease, sign analysis of first derivatives, and establishing functional inequalities. Questions regularly involve composite functions or functions defined by integrals using Leibniz's rule.

3. **Tangents & Normals (32 questions, 2019-2026; 48 total, last asked 2023)**
Dealt with equations of tangents and normals to curves, orthogonality of intersecting curves, and intercept lengths. This topic was officially removed from the syllabus starting in 2024, explaining why it was last asked in 2023.

4. **Rate of Change (9 questions, 2019-2026; 20 total, last asked 2025)**
Examines related rates problems involving physical models, such as expanding fluid volumes, melting spherical shells, or geometric shapes with changing dimensional parameters.

## What the questions look like

Recent problems in applications of derivatives frequently adhere to these standard paradigms:

- **Global Extremum on Closed Intervals**: You are given a high-degree polynomial or an algebraic difference on a closed interval. Finding the minimum or maximum requires comparing functional values at critical points against boundary values.
- **Monotonicity of Composite Functions**: Given a base function with specified convexity and derivative roots, you determine the intervals where a composite quadratic argument increases or decreases.
- **Integral-Defined Function Optimization**: The function is presented as an integral with variable limits containing a parameter. Applying Leibniz's differentiation rule yields the derivative, whose roots give candidate extreme points.
- **Inequalities Derived from Monotonicity**: Establishing that one function dominates another over a positive interval by constructing an auxiliary difference function and proving that its derivative remains strictly positive.

## How to practise it

Solving optimization and monotonicity questions cleanly requires sound algebraic discipline:

- **Check boundary endpoints systematically**: When asked for absolute maxima or minima on a closed interval, never stop at critical points where the derivative vanishes. Always compute values at the endpoints of the interval and compare them directly.
- **Examine non-differentiable points**: Critical points include locations where the first derivative does not exist, such as vertices of absolute value expressions. Test these corner points alongside stationary points.
- **Use first-derivative sign charts**: Rather than calculating complicated second derivatives to classify extrema, observe the sign changes of the first derivative around the stationary point. A sign shift from positive to negative indicates a local maximum, while negative to positive marks a local minimum.
- **Differentiate Leibniz integrals with care**: When finding extrema of an accumulation function, apply Leibniz's rule accurately by substituting the upper limit and multiplying by its derivative before subtracting the lower limit contribution.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the tangents and normals topic completely eliminated?
Tangents and normals to general curves were removed from the official JEE Main syllabus starting in 2024. Consequently, questions requiring equations of tangents to general curves no longer appear under differential calculus. However, tangent and normal properties of conic sections are still tested under coordinate geometry.

### How do I prove functional inequalities using derivatives?
To verify an inequality such as f(x) greater than g(x) for x greater than a given constant, construct an auxiliary function h(x) equal to f(x) minus g(x). Check the initial value at the starting boundary point. If the initial value is non-negative and the first derivative remains positive throughout the open domain, the function is strictly increasing, establishing the desired inequality.

### Why did this chapter's question frequency drop in recent years?
The removal of tangents, normals, and mean value theorems narrowed the syllabus scope for this chapter, leaving maxima, minima, and monotonicity as the active testing domains. Even so, these remaining concepts are regularly integrated into problems across definite integration and algebra. Step-by-step solutions for each one are in the EdLoop app.
