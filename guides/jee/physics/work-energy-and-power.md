## What the questions look like

Energy conservation problems frequently challenge students to read potential energy graphs. You will find plots of potential energy U(x) against position x where you must determine force directions from negative derivatives, or identify points of stable, unstable, and neutral equilibrium where slope vanishes. Other common questions evaluate work done using the work-energy theorem: given a polynomial expression for position with time, you compute velocities at the boundary times to find kinetic energy change directly rather than integrating forces over distance.

Collision questions split between perfectly elastic and partially or completely inelastic impacts. A classic problem sets up two spherical masses colliding along a line and sticking together, asking for the loss of mechanical energy or the consequent temperature increase of the combined mass. Oblique collision setups require separate restitution equations along the common normal and momentum conservation along the tangent.

Work and power problems test vector algebra and calculus. You will encounter questions calculating work done by multiple constant forces moving a mass along a specified displacement vector, or finding instantaneous power at a given timestamp for an accelerating mass driven by a time-varying vector force.

A major portion of this chapter is tested via direct calculation: 48 of the 151 questions between 2019 and 2026 were numerical answer items without multiple-choice options.

## How to practise it

Begin by drilling the work-energy theorem until it becomes your default problem-solving tool. Whenever a question asks for speed, height, or spring compression without mentioning time, try work-energy balance before writing down equations of motion. Remember that work done by all forces, including friction and applied external forces, equals the change in kinetic energy.

For potential energy functions, practice relating force to the spatial gradient: force in one dimension equals negative dU/dx. Check your signs carefully; a positive slope implies a force directed along the negative coordinate direction. For equilibrium points, evaluate second derivatives: positive curvature signifies stable equilibrium, while negative curvature indicates instability.

In collision problems, always establish coordinate axes along the line of impact and the transverse plane. Write momentum conservation along the line of impact, and write the coefficient of restitution formula as the ratio of separation speed to approach speed along that line alone. Verify your energy bookkeeping to ensure kinetic energy never increases in passive collisions.

### How do I tell whether to use momentum conservation or energy conservation?

Momentum is conserved whenever the net external force on the system along an axis is zero, regardless of whether mechanical energy is lost. Energy conservation applies only when non-conservative forces like friction or inelastic deformation do no net work.
