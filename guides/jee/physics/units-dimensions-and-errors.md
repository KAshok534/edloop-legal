## What the questions look like

Dimensional analysis questions generally follow two main formats. In the first format, you are given an unfamiliar physical relationship, such as an altered fluid flow formula or an electromagnetic expression, and asked to find the dimensions of unknown coefficients using dimensional homogeneity. In the second format, questions ask you to match physical quantities, often combining thermal constants, magnetic permeability, and electrical permittivity, against their dimensional expressions in terms of mass, length, time, and electric current.

Error questions usually center on laboratory formulas. A typical problem presents a cylinder, pendulum, or electrical resistor where each measurement carries a stated percentage uncertainty. You take logarithmic differentials to relate fractional errors, taking care to multiply exponents and add absolute fractional uncertainties. Significant figure problems ask for the correct decimal representation after adding lengths or calculating density from mass and volume.

Measuring instruments problems evaluate practical laboratory skills. You will determine the least count of a vernier scale where n vernier divisions coincide with n - 1 main scale divisions, or calculate the true thickness of a wire using a screw gauge whose circular scale has an initial zero offset.

The chapter also contains a steady proportion of numerical-response problems: 25 of the 235 questions since 2019 had no options, requiring clean arithmetic without relying on option choices.

## How to practise it

Begin by compiling a reference table of dimensional formulas for quantities outside basic mechanics. Focus on quantities that frequently recur in exam questions: Stefan's constant, universal gas constant, magnetic flux, self-inductance, and coefficient of viscosity. Derive each from a fundamental equation rather than memorizing bare powers.

Next, practise error propagation with powers and quotients. Train yourself to write the fractional error expression directly from the formula without intermediate steps, remembering that uncertainties always add in worst-case analysis. For significant figures, review the distinction between decimal places (used in addition and subtraction) and significant digits (used in multiplication and division).

For measuring instruments, draw the barrel and thimble scales of a screw gauge and the main and vernier jaws of calipers. Work through problems with negative zero error, ensuring you subtract the negative reading rather than adding it. Finish by timing yourself on mixed sets of recent questions.
