## What the questions look like

Calorimetry problems revolve around thermal equilibrium equations. A typical setup adds sub-zero ice to warm water inside a calorimeter of known heat capacity, asking for the final mixture temperature or the residual ice mass. Students must perform an energy check first: calculate the heat needed to warm the ice to 0 °C and melt it, compare this against the heat released when water cools to 0 °C, and determine whether all ice melts. Continuous flow problems ask for the fuel consumption rate of a domestic gas geyser warming a known volumetric flow of cold inlet water.

Conduction problems apply Ohm's law to heat currents. When several metal rods of different lengths, cross-sections, and thermal conductivities are joined in series or parallel between fixed thermal reservoirs, students write heat current conservation at internal junctions to deduce intermediate temperatures.

Thermal expansion problems evaluate differential elongation. When two bars of different metals are joined end-to-end and heated through a temperature rise, you sum their individual length increases. In radiation, problems compare emissive powers of spherical bodies using Stefan's law, where radiated energy scales with the surface area and the fourth power of absolute temperature.

A notable portion of problems requires exact values: 23 of the 91 questions between 2019 and 2026 were numerical answer items with no choices provided.

## How to practise it

Approach calorimetry by setting up an explicit energy balance ledger. Never write an equilibrium equation with an unknown final temperature until you have verified whether a phase transition completes. Calculate the energy required to reach the phase boundary first; if the available energy is insufficient to complete the latent heat exchange, the final temperature is locked at the transition point (such as 0 °C for an ice-water mixture).

In thermal conduction, convert each rod into an equivalent thermal resistance equal to length divided by the product of thermal conductivity and cross-sectional area. Treat temperature differences exactly like potential differences and heat flow rates like electric currents. Applying Kirchhoff's junction law at rod intersections makes finding interface temperatures painless.

For thermal radiation, remember that temperatures in the Stefan-Boltzmann law and Wien's displacement law must always be expressed in Kelvin. Convert degrees Celsius to absolute temperatures before raising them to the fourth power. For Newton's law of cooling, remember that it holds only for small temperature differences above ambient surroundings.

### How does thermal resistance simplify composite slab problems?

By treating a thermal slab as a resistor with resistance L / (k A), series slabs simply add their resistances, while parallel slabs combine reciprocally, allowing you to bypass multi-variable differential equations.
