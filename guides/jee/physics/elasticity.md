## What the questions look like

Modulus questions primarily focus on wire stretching and comparative ratios. A common problem compares two metal wires made of different materials, with given ratios of original length and cross-sectional area, subjected to identical stretching forces or experiencing identical elongations. Students equate tensile stress to Young's modulus multiplied by longitudinal strain to solve for unknown dimensional or material ratios. Other questions evaluate volumetric compression in liquids, where you calculate percentage volume decrease using bulk modulus and applied pressure.

Stress-strain curve problems test qualitative and quantitative understanding of material behavior. Given graphs of strain plotted against stress for multiple substances, you must identify the material possessing the highest Young's modulus by examining reciprocal slopes, or distinguish brittle materials from ductile metals based on their plastic deformation ranges.

Elastic energy problems calculate the work stored inside deformed materials. Standard setups evaluate elastic potential energy stored in a wire under load, or compute the temperature drop required to induce a specified thermal tension in a clamped rod whose thermal contraction is restrained. Another regular problem type evaluates tensile stress at intermediate cross-sections along a heavy suspended rope bearing its own self-weight plus an external load.

Numerical-response problems form an unusually heavy slice of this chapter: 42 of the 99 questions from 2019 to 2026 had no answer choices, making meticulous unit tracking essential.

## How to practise it

Start by mastering the three primary elastic moduli: Young's modulus for longitudinal stretch, shear modulus for shape distortion at constant volume, and bulk modulus for volumetric compression. Write out each definition clearly, ensuring you recognize that strain is a dimensionless ratio while stress and moduli carry units of pressure (N/m² or Pa).

Pay special attention to axes when reading graphical data. Standard physics textbooks place stress on the vertical axis and strain on the horizontal axis, where slope directly yields Young's modulus. However, JEE Main frequently inverts the axes by plotting strain on the vertical axis and stress on the horizontal axis; in such cases, the steepest curve corresponds to the smallest Young's modulus rather than the largest.

For elastic potential energy, practice deriving both total stored energy (half of force times elongation) and energy density (half of stress times strain). When solving thermal stress questions, recognize that prevented thermal contraction equals mechanical elongation, so thermal stress simply equals Young's modulus times the linear expansion coefficient times temperature change.

### How does thermal stress arise in a clamped metal rod?

When a metal rod is clamped rigidly at both ends and cooled, thermal contraction is prevented. The clamps must exert outward tensile forces to hold the length fixed, setting up an internal tensile stress equal to the product of Young's modulus, the linear expansion coefficient, and the temperature drop.
